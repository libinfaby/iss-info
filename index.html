About the ISS

The International Space Station is a modular space station in low Earth orbit - the largest crewed object ever assembled in space. Orbiting at approximately 408 km altitude, it completes a full revolution of Earth every 90 minutes at 28,000 km/h.

A collaborative project between NASA, Roscosmos, ESA, JAXA, and CSA, the ISS has maintained a continuous human presence since November 2000. Its pressurized modules host cutting-edge research in biology, physics, astronomy, and meteorology - experiments only possible in the microgravity environment of orbit. Read more at NASA →

Questions
Can I see the ISS without a telescope?

Yes. It is one of the brightest objects in the night sky and looks like a steady white light moving smoothly from one side of the sky to the other in a few minutes. It does not blink and makes no sound.

Why can't I see it every night?

You can only see it when it passes over your area while your sky is dark and the station itself is still lit by the Sun. That happens shortly after sunset or before sunrise. Passes in daylight or in Earth's shadow are listed under All passes but cannot be seen.

How often does it go around Earth?

About once every 93 minutes, which is 15 to 16 times a day. The crew see a sunrise and a sunset on almost every orbit.

Why does the track on the map look like a wave?

The orbit is a circle tilted 51.6° to the equator. Drawn on a flat map it becomes a wave, and because Earth turns underneath, each orbit crosses the ground a little further west than the last.

How accurate are the positions and pass times?

Positions are calculated in your browser from the station's published orbital elements, refreshed several times a day. They are normally right to within a few kilometres and pass times to within a few seconds. Accuracy drops for a while after the station fires its engines to adjust its orbit.
